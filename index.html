How to calibrate

Phones differ in how long their audio takes to reach the speaker and mic, so sonar needs one calibration against a distance you measured yourself. It is saved on this device and only needs repeating if you change phone, speaker, or frequency by a lot.

  1. Set the air temperature in the box below.

  2. Face a flat, hard surface. Stand the phone on a table or hold it still about 1 m (3 ft) from a wall, door or cabinet. Point the bottom edge, where the speaker and mic are, straight at it. Keep the space between them clear.

  3. Measure the distance from the phone's bottom edge to the surface with a tape measure, and enter it in the box above (50 to 300 cm).

    About 100 cm works well. The more exact the tape measure, the more accurate every reading afterwards.

  4. Stay still and quiet, then press Run calibration. It plays a series of pings for about 4 seconds. Nothing should move between the phone and the surface.

  5. Check it. Press Start with the phone still aimed at that surface. The nearest echo should read within a couple of centimetres of your tape measure. If not, check the measurement and run it again.

Doppler needs no distance. It only learns the room's background: the quiet shimmer around the tone when nothing is moving. That takes about 2.5 seconds, and it happens automatically each time you press Start. Hold the phone and everything in front of it still while it runs. Press Relearn baseline if readings drift or the room changes.

Sound travels faster in warm air, which slightly changes every distance and speed. Enter the temperature of the room you are in.

Frequency 18 kHz

The pitch of the ping or tone, in kHz. Lower values (16 to 18) are louder and carry further, but some people can hear them. Higher values (19 to 21) are less noticeable, but many phone speakers and mics respond weakly up there. If readings are weak, try a lower one.

ms between pings

How often a ping is sent. Continuous pings as fast as the max range allows, about 10 times a second, which suits tracking something that moves. 1000 ms or 2000 ms gives one reading every second or two, which is easier to read and lighter on the battery. Custom accepts any interval from 100 ms.

How far away the sonar listens for echoes. A shorter range pings faster and gives a cleaner trace. A longer range waits for echoes from further away.

How strong an echo must be to count. Raise it to catch weak or distant echoes. Lower it if you see readings that are not real.

How large a change must be to count as motion. Raise it to react to small movements. Lower it if the room's noise triggers false motion.

How loud the ping or tone is. Also set the phone's own volume to about 70%. Near-ultrasound can be audible to children and pets, so turn it down around them.

Advanced

Which speaker plays the ping or tone. Only one plays at a time. The bottom speaker is best, because it sits next to the microphone.

Phones with two speakers, such as iPhones, play the left channel from one speaker and the right from the other. Pick one side so only one speaker is used. Which side is the bottom speaker depends on how you hold the phone, so test in the same orientation you will use and keep rotation locked.

A short, low-volume 600 Hz beep, so you can hear where each channel comes from. If you hear nothing, raise the phone's volume a little and check that the silent switch is off.

How wide a range of pitches each ping sweeps across. A wider sweep tells apart objects that are close together: about 9 cm apart at 2000 Hz. A narrower sweep reaches further but only separates objects about 34 cm apart at 500 Hz. The default of 1000 Hz is a balance.

Echoes closer than this are ignored. The speaker's sound leaks straight into the mic and would show up as a fake object right at the phone. Raise it if a phantom reading stays near zero. Lower it only if you need to measure very close up.

How long a slice of sound is analysed at a time. Fast reacts quickly but cannot tell similar speeds apart. Fine separates speeds more precisely but responds more slowly. Changing it relearns the baseline.

Sonar: after calibrating once, aim the phone's speaker and mic at a surface and press Start. Doppler: hold the phone still, let it learn the baseline, then move a hand or body toward or away from it.
